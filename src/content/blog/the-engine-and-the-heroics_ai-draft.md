Most of the growth I see in small companies runs on heroics.

A founder who closes every important deal himself. Two engineers who carry the whole product on their backs. One person in operations who knows where all the bodies are buried and quietly keeps the thing from falling over. When the quarter comes in, everyone exhales and says we did it.

And they did. But look closely at how.

I have a line I use when a team starts obsessing over the wrong thing. You have to keep gas in the car and your tires rotated, but the goal is to get to Chicago by tomorrow night. Focus on the drive, not the tuning. Heroics are the opposite of that. Heroics are a group of talented people personally dragging the car down the highway because nobody ever built an engine.

It works. Right up until it doesn't.

## Heroics feel like success

Heroics feel like success because the scoreboard agrees. Revenue showed up. The launch shipped. The fire got put out. The problem is that none of it is repeatable, and none of it survives the people who pulled it off having a bad month, taking a vacation, or walking out the door.

I've watched companies try to fight professional armies with a band of volunteers carrying farm muskets. Everyone is working hard. Everyone is talented. And they keep losing, because effort is not a system. The other side isn't more heroic. They built the engine and stopped depending on heroes.

## The boring version of growth

An engine is the boring version of growth. It's the repeatable path a stranger could walk and still get a result. The sales motion that works when your best closer is out. The onboarding that doesn't hinge on one person remembering the twelve steps. The weekly rhythm that surfaces a problem on Tuesday instead of in next quarter's postmortem.

When you have an engine, growth gets predictable. Predictable is the whole game. Predictable is what lets you make a hire before you're drowning, sign a longer lease, tell an investor a number you actually believe. The companies that win the scary seasons aren't the ones who worked the hardest. They're the ones who, when things got tight, protected the engine instead of asking their people to be heroes a little longer.

That's also why you can't save your way to success. When cash gets tight, the reflex is to cut. Trim the spend, pause the hiring, kill anything without a revenue number attached yet. It feels responsible, and for a quarter it buys you air. Then you look up and the actual problem is still sitting there, except now the thing that was supposed to create growth is smaller. Cutting buys time. Time is not a strategy.

Building the engine is the strategy. Even in a tight season. Especially in a tight season.

## The test

Here's the test I'd give any founder who thinks they're growing. Take a week off. Don't check in. If the number still moves, you have an engine. If it stalls the second you step away, you don't have a company yet. You have a job you happen to own, and a very tired group of heroes.

That's hard to hear when the heroics are working. But the heroics working is exactly the trap. It feels like proof you've built something, when really it's proof you're still the thing holding it together.

None of this means heroes don't matter. Early on, heroics are the only thing you've got, and you should be grateful for the people willing to drag the car. The job is to use the runway those people buy you to build the engine that replaces the dragging. Heroes get you to the next quarter. Engines get you to Chicago.

Build the engine. Then go take the week off and see if it holds.
