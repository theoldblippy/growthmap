Every operator I know has the same reflex. Something breaks, and we run at it with a hose.

A customer's unhappy. A report's late. A handoff between two teams is a mess again, and the dashboard just turned a color you don't like. Each one feels urgent, and each one is a chance to be useful, so we grab it. By Friday we've put out a dozen fires and moved the business exactly nowhere.

I've lived that week more times than I want to admit. It feels like leadership. It's mostly motion.

There's a line I keep coming back to, from Reid Hoffman by way of a mentor of mine: in a fast-growing company, everything is broken all the time. If you wait for the day nothing is on fire before you focus, that day never comes. So the actual job stops being "put out fires" and becomes something harder. Decide which ones you let burn.

I said it to my team once, half as a joke and half as a survival strategy. Get used to the smoke, boys. Not every fire is yours to fight. Some of them burn out on their own. Some of them don't matter. And a few are burning in a corner of the building nobody important ever visits.

**The filter**

Here's how I decide now. Every fire gets two questions.

First: will putting this out move my top-line number? Not "is it annoying," not "would it be nice," not "does someone on the team keep bringing it up." Will it actually move revenue, or the closest leading indicator to revenue you've got. Most fires fail this question immediately.

Second: if it won't move the number, does it genuinely threaten the business or a person? A security hole threatens the business. A great employee about to quit is a person. Those get attention whether or not they show up on the scoreboard, because the cost of ignoring them is real and it compounds.

If a fire is a no on both, it burns. You walk past it. You feel the itch to fix it, and you walk past it anyway.

That itch is the hard part. Fixing things feels good, especially the small ones, because you get a clean win in twenty minutes. Reorganizing the folders, rewriting the doc nobody reads, smoothing over the mildly annoyed teammate. Real progress on the thing that matters is slow and uncomfortable and doesn't give you that hit. So we drift toward the small fires and call it discipline.

**Why this is really a growth problem**

I used to think this was a time-management problem. Now I think it's a growth problem, the difference between a business that grows on purpose and one that grows by accident, if it grows at all.

Every company has one real constraint at a time. One rate limiter. For most of the companies I work with, it's some version of "not enough people are buying." Everything upstream and downstream of that constraint is noise until the constraint moves. When you let yourself fight every fire, you spread your best hours evenly across problems that do not deserve equal weight. The constraint gets the same slice of you as the typo.

Letting fires burn is how you claw those hours back and aim them at the one thing. It's unglamorous and it's the whole ballgame.

There's a cousin to this that shows up everywhere: trying to save your way to success. When money gets tight, the instinct is to cut. Cutting has its place. It keeps you alive. But you have never once watched a company shrink its way to greatness. The spreadsheet of expenses to trim is just another stack of small fires that feel productive while the constraint sits untouched. Alive and winning are two different things.

**The test that keeps me honest**

The version of this I like best came from Ryan Deiss. He calls it the vacation test. If you left your business for thirty days, no laptop, no calls, what would break?

The first time I ran it on myself the list was long. Way too long. And most of it was stuff that only worked because I was the one holding it together. For years I'd quietly filed that under "being important." It's really just a pile of fires I never let burn, plus a bunch of systems I never built because fighting the fire myself was faster in the moment.

A business that needs you for everything is a very demanding job you can never quit, dressed up as an asset. The ones that grow predictably are almost boring when the founder steps away. Nothing catches fire, because the things that would have caught fire were either handled by a system or allowed to burn out a long time ago.

So that's the work. Pick the one constraint. Build the systems around it so it keeps moving whether or not you're in the room. And for everything else, get comfortable with a little smoke in the air. It smells like focus.
