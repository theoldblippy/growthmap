I run my teams off three colors.

I call it stoplight management. Red, yellow, green. If it's green, I don't care. If it's yellow, what's the plan to get back to green? If it's red, what's the plan to get back to yellow?

That's the whole system. It fits on an index card, and it does more work than most of the expensive operating frameworks I've watched companies install and quietly abandon six months later.

## The problem it solves

Most status meetings are theater. You go around the room, every person reads their update out loud, and 80% of what gets said is about work that's fine. Nobody makes a decision. The meeting ends and the two things that actually needed attention got seven minutes between them.

The scarce resource in a leadership meeting is attention. When you treat every number as equally worth discussing, the number that's quietly falling apart gets the same airtime as the one that's crushing it. Usually less, because the person who owns the failing number would rather talk about something else.

Stoplight management is an attention router. Green means on track, and we skip it. Yellow means off track, and the owner brings a plan to get back to green. Red means seriously off track, and the owner brings a plan to get back to yellow.

Notice red's job. You don't ask a red metric to be green next week. You ask it to become yellow. Progress in one honest step.

## Setting it up

Three moves.

First, pick the numbers. Five to ten per team, and every one of them should be a number someone would fight to defend. If doubling a metric tomorrow wouldn't change your top-line result, it doesn't earn a row on the scoreboard.

Second, define the colors ahead of time. This is the part everyone skips, and it's the part that makes the system honest. Green means at or above plan. Yellow means below plan but recoverable this quarter. Red means below plan and the current approach won't recover it. Write the thresholds down before the quarter starts, while nobody knows which metrics will be embarrassing. If you set thresholds after you see the numbers, you'll negotiate with yourself every week and everything will come out green.

Third, run the meeting off the colors. Thirty minutes. Greens get read as a list, ten seconds. Yellows and reds get the rest of the time, and the only question on the table is the plan. Who owns it, what changes this week, when do we expect the color to move.

## Where it breaks

The failure mode is an all-green scoreboard in a company everyone can feel is struggling. I've sat in that room. The board says green and the bank account says otherwise. When that happens, either the thresholds are mush or your people are scared, and both of those are your problem as the leader, because both are things you built.

A coach of mine has a phrase for the fix: lean into the red. Make red a signal that help is on the way. The first time someone marks their own metric red and gets resources, your scoreboard starts telling the truth. The first time someone gets torched for it, you will never see an honest red again.

One more habit that helps. When a metric goes yellow, resist the urge to solve it in the room. The owner brings the plan. Your job is to pressure test it and clear the path. If you grab the wheel every time a color changes, you teach the team that yellow means "Dad takes over," and they'll hide problems to keep the keys.

## Why bother

Because this is how you go from luck to running a business.

Growth you can't see coming is luck, and luck is a terrible thing to build a payroll on. A scoreboard with honest colors tells you where next quarter comes from while there's still time to do something about it.

It also does something for you personally. When the greens are trustworthy, you can actually leave them alone. That's the start of a business that runs without you standing over it, which is the only version of a business worth building.

Red, yellow, green. Start next Monday.
