I spent two weeks recently trying to figure out why a sales number was broken.

The instinct, everywhere, is to go study the losses. Pull the deals that died, line them up, look for the common cause of death. It feels productive. It feels rigorous. Every company I've worked in runs some version of the loss autopsy.

Somewhere in the middle of it I realized we were doing the whole thing backwards.

The losses were telling us a hundred different stories. This one died on price. This one died because the champion left. This one went dark for no reason anyone could name. You can spend a month sorting that pile and walk away with a list of fourteen things to fix, which is another way of saying you walk away with nothing to fix.

The wins told one story.

When we pulled the deals that actually closed and asked what they had in common, a pattern showed up almost immediately. A specific kind of buyer, in the room, already half-sold before we ever showed up. A cycle that closed in thirty days or less. A couple of other conditions that kept repeating. And here's the part that made me sit up: when all of those things were present, we won almost every time. When even one of them was missing, the win rate fell off a cliff.

Call that what it is. A recipe.

## Losses scatter, wins converge

Here's the thing nobody tells you about post-mortems. Losses are high-variance. A deal can die for a reason that will never repeat, caused by a person who no longer works there, in a quarter that looked nothing like this one. You can learn something from a loss, but you're learning it one deal at a time, and half of what you learn doesn't generalize.

Wins are the opposite. A repeatable win, by definition, repeats. If you can find the three or four conditions that show up every time you close, you've found the thing you can actually manufacture. You can build a motion around it. You can staff to it. You can tell a rep exactly what a good deal looks like before they waste a month on a bad one.

Studying losses tells you what to avoid. Studying wins tells you what to build. Only one of those two things compounds.

## What a win actually looks like

The exercise itself is almost embarrassingly simple, which is probably why so few teams do it.

Take your last ten or twenty clean wins. Not the flukes, not the ones that closed because someone owed you a favor. The real ones. Then write down, in plain language, what was true about each of them. Who was the buyer. How long did it take. What had to happen before they said yes. What did the first conversation feel like. What did you do that you don't always do.

You are looking for the conditions that keep showing up. When you find them, write the sentence out loud: "When these things are true, we win almost every time. When one of them is missing, we usually don't."

Now you have something you can hand to another human being. That last part matters more than it sounds. A win that lives in your best rep's gut belongs to the rep, not the company. When that person leaves, the win leaves with them. A win you've written down in enough detail that a new hire could recognize it is an asset the business owns.

This is the whole game, honestly. The difference between a company that grows on purpose and one that grows by luck is whether it can describe its own wins well enough to repeat them.

## Growth on purpose

I said something to a team a couple of weeks ago that I keep coming back to. We're not going to get there by accident, and we're not going to get there by working on fifteen projects at once.

You don't stumble into a great quarter. Somebody, somewhere, figured out what a win looks like, pointed the whole team at producing more of exactly that, and then got a little obsessed with the number that told them whether it was working. That's it. That's the machine.

Most teams never build it because building it requires admitting you don't already know why you win. That's uncomfortable. It's much easier to keep running loss autopsies, because losses give you someone to blame and something to fix, and both of those feel like progress.

Take your wins apart instead. Find the pattern. Write it down so plainly that a stranger could spot it. Then go build more of it, on purpose, and watch the one number that tells you it's working.

The teams that do this stop hoping for good quarters. They start manufacturing them.
